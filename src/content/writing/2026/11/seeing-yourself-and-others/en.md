The social-media show *I Miss You* has been appearing in my feed a lot over the past two weeks, so I watched some of it. Watching a show takes up only a small part of an ordinary day, but this one stayed with me. I wanted to write about human feelings beyond AI, and to record how I have been learning to cherish the life and feelings I have now.

## Seeing

There is a lot of genuine feeling in the show. Xu Fei and Li Na moved me most. What they have does not seem like purely platonic friendship. There may be a love that has changed into something else, perhaps even something familial. The honest words and release of emotion depend largely on Xu Fei's courage. She said so many things out loud, which I found remarkable. She was willing to put herself on trial in a public show in order to make peace. If you want to reconcile, you have to be willing to give everything you can to make it happen.

Each pair of guests who have fallen out has their own problems, struggles, habits, and conflicts. Everyone is an individual. When we are together, we often assume the other person is just like us. In fact, they are someone entirely different. This happens all the time: in our relationships, we often see only ourselves and fail to see the other person.

One interesting thing about the show is that people are willing to open up, some sincerely, others less so. Some are also willing to observe and offer an outside perspective, helping the guests see how they differ and loosen some knots. Zhang Quanling uses many gentle metaphors to describe the guests' states of mind and how they approach other people. She helps them find the words for things they have struggled to say.

The show lets viewers see who these people are, at their most unguarded, within their friendships. Some are devoted; some see only themselves; some may even act with malice. All of that is ordinary. What I see more often is people stuck in their difficulties. I rarely see someone who truly sees the other person. But people are also strange in a good way: with a third party reflecting their differences and showing each of them where they are stuck, they may come to understand each other and suddenly see a way through. There are uncomfortable parts of the show too, including deliberate editing and prompting. Still, it reveals something good, and shows how youth and friendship shape a person.

While watching, I also saw many clips and recommendations. With today's content creation full of short dramas and AI, why can a work like this still spread so widely and resonate with so many people? Some say sincere emotion will always have more power than the fantasies AI offers. Others have a different explanation: when entertainment no longer has a cultural or entertainment function of its own, it starts consuming real human emotion instead. I think both explanations have something to them. The show does seem to be a postmodern form of entertainment consumption, perhaps one of the few things that can still resonate so strongly.

I recently came across another account of how people come to identify with something. The long videos Douyin has been making, and creators' content about companionship and personal growth, are essentially doing the same thing: expressing something viewers recognize in themselves but have not felt seen for. It seems to work on two levels. First, viewers see themselves and think, "That's exactly where I am right now." Second, they feel they might become more open-minded, or find an answer for the future. It does not necessarily have to be about becoming better. Improvement is not the driving force; the appeal is the feeling that this might help them get away from something. This emotional resonance and guidance, and the consumer economy around it, resemble the old practices of praying to immortals and Buddhas, fortune-telling, and systems like MBTI. People assign themselves to a category and look for a general answer: if someone in the past walked a similar path, perhaps that person has an answer for me too.

The earlier relationship show that drew the most attention was *See You Again*. It had all kinds of people in it, and it is hard to judge. I found it more infuriating because it showed so much arrogance and blindness toward other people. At least the guests on *I Miss You* seem to know they are there to reconcile. That show is more about seeing yourself and others from different perspectives.

Friendship resonates because everyone has experienced it. But much of the response comes from seeing that experience described, from feeling recognized. People are, for the most part, good. They become estranged because they can see only themselves. When they manage to see each other again, the warmth and sincerity of that moment are precious. What makes it precious is how much they value and cherish the friendship. People can express genuine feelings in words, and those words can resonate with others. That is another reason those feelings deserve to be cherished.

I have also been wondering how to write about human feeling. I thought of an older colleague from a previous job. His writing was delicate, a little like Wang Zengqi's, full of plain description and memories of daily life: details of cooking, the tastes of food, and the small things one remembers. So much feeling lived in those details. I once put together a literary journal issue for him, and he wrote me a farewell piece too. He put a great deal of affection into words. There was also a younger colleague at my former company who wrote an essay called "If Only Life Were Stardew Valley." He told some of his own stories, and many people found their memories of the past in them. In the end, real human feeling matters. What resonates is the feeling itself, including our identification with certain values, events, and people.

It is hard to fully understand another person, and understanding and recognition between people are both difficult. But expressing similar feelings more plainly may help. Words can sometimes feel inadequate; still, a detail rendered in simple, exact strokes can carry memories and emotions across time and place.

As I write this, I feel that my writing has been getting worse. Looking back on these months away from work, I have not read much. I have read plenty of material, but my focus has been too narrow, mostly product research. Lately I have been thinking about how to read more books. I need to make time for a wider range of ideas. I have also felt that all my conversations with AI lately leave little human feeling behind. Knowledge passes through my mind like water, but does not leave much there.

Coming back to my own life, the show reminded me of something similar between my best friend and me: years of misunderstanding and reconciliation, our happiest moments and the times we hurt each other. It took a lot of time and effort to make peace and find a way to be together that paid more attention to each other and to life. There was no third party to guide us through it. I had to make sense of complicated things and understand people on my own, which is hard to do.

That made me feel how rare it is to resolve the difficulties between people, especially in a friendship, and to have the chance to speak honestly to each other. We often find good friends because of who we are and because we shared a particular stage of life. When that stage passes and the things we have in common change, some feelings gradually fade. Some relationships are deliberately maintained, of course, but most become as bland as plain water. People get stuck here too. In a relationship, each person is trying to meet a need for self-expression and self-regard, to prove their worth. That is where things snag: the closer the friends, the more likely they are, when caught up in their own troubles, to see only their own needs and miss the other's. Or their circumstances may leave them unable to recognize and understand what the other needs. Or they may use their own assumptions to guess what the other person will do, then wait for something that never comes. These are serious problems. It may also be that friendship is valuable simply for the feeling itself. In that case, trying to repair it depends more on human affection and less on anything else.

What I want to write about, then, is mutual understanding: how one person comes to understand another. It is genuinely hard, like the question Zhuangzi and Huizi argued over at the Hao River: "You are not a fish. How do you know what gives fish pleasure?"

To understand a wide range of people, a person may need to have suffered enough. Otherwise, it is hard to empathize with different feelings and experiences or to put yourself in someone else's place. Perhaps there are genuinely gifted people, precocious from a young age, who can understand a wide range of experiences and feelings. Most of us, though, can manage what actors call experiential acting, while few can do method acting. That is a reminder that method acting can be effective, but should be approached with care.

I once put it this way: I see all my hardships and growth, along with everything I cannot express, cannot understand, or have never had. Together, they make me who I am. They also let me see the person I am speaking with: how their experiences and growth have shaped them, the difficulty they are in, what they care about and what they do not, what they like and what they fear. I can understand their motives and struggles, and I know how to plant a tree or pull up a seedling too soon. When I see myself whole, I have judged myself, but I do not intend to judge anyone else. At the beginning of *The Great Gatsby*, Nick's father tells him: "Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone, just remember that all the people in this world haven't had the advantages that you've had."

Returning to *I Miss You*, I think Zhang Quanling's way of speaking resembles mine at times: see the other person, find the words they can understand, and express what you see. Sometimes that means reaching for a more romantic metaphor, like a sunflower and its roots, or two lotus flowers growing from the same stem. This is a skill, and a form of empathy. More than anything, it means finding a precise enough metaphor to put another person's sense of self and feelings into words.

But seeing is only half of it. The other half is expression. Friendship matters. When two people share a bond that matters, they need to see each other and say some things out loud. We cannot assume the other person understands us completely. Each of us understands only ourselves, and expression always leaves things out. It can take many tries and a lot of effort to piece the words together and make ourselves clear. If we want to reconcile, we also need to express ourselves in a way the other person can understand and feel, rather than treating them as if they were just like us and would naturally understand our silence or our unspoken assumptions. The fox in *The Little Prince* says that language is the source of misunderstandings. If I have not made you feel my affection for you, then I have not yet understood how you receive it. That is something I still have to learn.

In the end, everyone is working through the difficulties of their own life, including what was missing or hard to understand as they grew up. Some recent conversations of mine also touched on my old work life. It is curious: lately, many of the people who have reached out are former colleagues. Perhaps we worked together for so long that, as things have changed for each of us, they have things to tell me and I have been there to listen. What is especially curious is that quite a few have come to talk when visiting the Bay Area on business.

One older colleague was here on a business trip, and we had dinner together. We talked about changes in our old department, then about his child studying in Beijing and buying an apartment in Xierqi for the school district. Another younger colleague had published a paper and come to the US for a conference. He talked more about the drive and confidence of youth, and feeling peer pressure to work even harder and compete. A third, a younger colleague from my startup days, had gone through major changes at work and came to tell me how he felt. We used to think that startup project could go on forever. We worked on it for years; I left first, and now he has moved on to something else. We also talked about his daughter. She was born only recently, and the parents are barely sleeping.

Another conversation was with someone here in the Bay Area whom I had mentored as an intern five or six years ago. He now works at a very well-known company. We had dinner in June, our first time meeting in person. He had done his undergraduate degree in the US and had interned in China during a school break when I hired him. As we walked and talked this time, he offered a strange opinion: people working on AI in the Bay Area would need at least ten million dollars to feel free. I remember thinking, are people working on AI here really that anxious? Maybe his view represents what some people think.

I have written all this simply to say that everyone seems caught in their own world, and has to find their own way out. When I talk with colleagues who are still working, I can see that their work worries may matter a great deal to them. It is normal for colleagues to talk about these things. Everyone is living inside their own story. Since I have left the workplace, I cannot do much to help. When people come to talk about work, I mostly listen and offer what understanding I still have without being too far removed from it. For friends who care deeply about their work and have ideals, my main contribution is to give them a bit of encouragement.

## Cherishing

I have spent all this time looking at other people. Now, back to myself. My wife and I have been living together in the Bay Area. Our days are ordinary: exercise, housework, cooking. What matters is cherishing this time together and making more memories through things we experience side by side.

Earlier, I still went out every day, taking the bus to Palo Alto to check in, then taking it back. I was not really going to work; I was just going for a change of scene. But the round trip was fun. What I enjoyed was taking the bus itself, not the time spent riding it. Before leaving, I would check when the bus was coming. I had to time the transfers too. Sometimes I could take the free Link shuttle, and on the way back I might remember to pick up groceries or pastries. It was a little like Wang Ziyou taking a boat through the snow to visit Dai Andao, then turning back when he reached the door: he went because the mood took him, and returned when it had passed.

These days I look at the sky more than anything else. There is a sunrise and sunset every day. When my schedule is regular, I can catch the sunrise; I see the sunset often. California sunsets are beautiful. Sometimes I see one at the beach, sometimes just by looking up outside our home, and sometimes from the car as the sun slowly sinks. The horizon turns red, pink, or purple. Sometimes there are clouds like phoenix feathers, alongside white lines left by planes. China has sunsets like this too, but I was too busy at work to go out and watch. Now I have more time, so I spend more of it looking at the sky. In summer it stayed light until seven or eight. Now, at the end of fall and start of winter, the clocks have changed and it is dark shortly after five-thirty. The night brings a cold that feels almost frosty.

The leaves are changing too. Many trees in the South Bay stay green, but around Mountain View and Stanford there are old trees, fifty or sixty years old, that have grown enormous. Whole streets that were lush green in summer are now lined with red leaves, beautiful when the sun falls on them. A couple of days ago I went out and noticed that the ginkgo leaves had turned almost entirely yellow. I remembered seeing the ginkgoes turn yellow in Beijing last year. Over just two or three days, a strong wind blew the golden leaves to the ground. Two days later, the trees that had been bright with gold were reduced to a few bare branches.

Some time ago, we went to San Francisco together. We had planned to check out a Filipino cultural festival, but it was so small it covered only one street. We walked around for two minutes and left. Later we went to Lombard Street. Parking was difficult, so we took the bus. The stop was three blocks away, and we had a long hill to climb, but we saw a beautiful moon. On the way up, I could not see it, only the sunset behind us. Halfway there, a huge moon suddenly appeared above the hill, against a dim sky turning blue, its light spreading through the clouds and across the sky. That enormous moon hung over San Francisco. From the top of Lombard Street, the sky looked deep blue, almost dark, but very clear. We watched the sky shift gradually from the red of sunset to blue, then take on a little purple. Seeing the sunset and moonrise together brought to mind Su Shi's *Night Tour at Chengtian Temple*: "What night is without a moon? Where is there no bamboo or cypress? It is only that few are as idle as the two of us."

The hydrangeas on Lombard Street were past their best by then. Many had faded in October. They look much better in sunlight; in moonlight, they seemed ready to fall asleep. The best hydrangeas I have seen were in Kamakura. In early summer 2024, I traveled to Tokyo for work and went to Meigetsu-in with a classmate who was doing a PhD at the University of Tokyo. Meigetsu-in is a temple. In Japanese, hydrangeas are called *ajisai*, a name said to come from Bai Juyi's poem: "Though no one in this world knows it, I give you the name purple-yang flower." Later we went to Enoshima to see the sea. I have seen quite a few seas over the years: the Pacific from Enoshima, the Indian Ocean in Bali, and the eastern edge of the Pacific here in California.

What I do most in daily life lately is cook. My cooking has steadily improved, and I have become rather good at making chili oil for dipping sauce. I had tried making sweet-and-sour ribs once or twice before and messed them up, but recently got them right. First I blanch the ribs with a little cooking wine and two slices of ginger. Once the blood and foam come out, I skim it off, take the ribs out, and dry each piece with a paper towel. Then I fry them in an iron pan until they smell dry and fragrant, pour in a sauce mixed to the right proportions, and add plenty of rock sugar and vinegar. I simmer them for an hour or two. At the end, when reducing the sauce, I add a little more aged vinegar and sesame seeds and toss everything together. Timing matters, and so does the heat. It is better to stick to the traditional seasoning ratio instead of improvising. The same goes for steamed pork. Pork in the US has a slight smell; blanching and drying it first makes that less noticeable. I have tried some new cuisines too. I cook what I like and put it on the table. We both eat happily, and that feels good to me. Wang Zengqi wrote in "Winter," "Family sitting idly together, lamplight close and warm." That is probably what he meant.

We also have little Maomao, a Siberian kitten. She is stubborn but truly adorable, and her expression changes every day. When she is happy, her face and eyes are round. When she is not, she looks thoroughly put out. She sleeps a lot and does not eat much, so I worry she will not grow. At the same time, I hope she grows very big. She is a forest cat, after all, and ought to become a giant cat. We recently bought two pots of plants, though I keep forgetting to water them. It has rained a little in the Bay Area lately, so perhaps they will be all right.

I have also talked with quite a few people around here. Most of my conversations have actually been with insurance companies on the phone, then with banks, and, after that, with people at the supermarket when I am looking for something.

We have been trying to buy Canadian icewine lately, and went to three or four Trader Joe's stores without finding it. At the third store, an employee said it would be in the next day. When we went back, we asked someone to look in the stockroom, and they found only one of the kinds we wanted. A few days later, the other kind still was not on the shelf. This time, when we asked an employee, he pulled it out from behind the counter. It took some effort to buy the wine, but it is very good. One of the older male employees was amusing and told us how to drink it. At checkout, a woman cashier said everyone at the store had bought some to take home too. She recommended putting it in the freezer for 30 minutes before drinking.

The other rambling bit might be my complaint that food in the Bay Area is mediocre. Many restaurants seem worth visiting only once every few months, or even six months. One time, though, we queued for an hour at an Italian pizza place in San Francisco. It was quite good. Still, I think the best pizza was in New York, during our Christmas trip last winter. It was winter there too, with a wind that cut right through us, and the days grew dark very early. We took a bus to a traditional Italian pizza place. It was small and simply decorated. We queued outside for a long time, then finally got a hot pizza and ate it right there by the door. It was a very thin, traditional Italian pizza, topped with chopped parsley, chili flakes, and a little salt. It really was delicious, though perhaps we were just very hungry by then.

So lately, what I find most interesting is life itself: paying attention to life, to what we want, and to the world the two of us can see together. I remembered that four years ago, when one of my direct reports left the company, he gave everyone on the team a gift. He gave me a copy of *Walden*, saying he thought I was too busy and needed to slow down. At the time, I wondered when I would ever slow my pace. Looking at the life and feelings I have now, I realize this is some of the rarest and most precious time in a person's life.

Compared with the pace of my life before, someone who is purely utilitarian or a workaholic would find it hard to spend time paying attention to life. Being able to turn your attention toward life makes every day feel fortunate. The things most worth cherishing in a lifetime are time, friendship, love, and the concrete moments of each day: walking hand in hand, going out to see something new, taking in an unfamiliar view, talking about nothing in particular, eating a good meal, or making a new dish.

Cherishing life has more to do with being a particular person and doing particular things than with staying inside a system or a tool-driven way of thinking.

## Closing

The piece has ended up a little scattered, almost like a running diary. I meant to write about why AI cannot produce feelings as full as human feelings. By the end, I may have written instead about the human life AI cannot experience.

I will end with a feeling unique to human beings. In *The Brothers Karamazov*, Dostoevsky writes: "Love life itself more than its meaning."
