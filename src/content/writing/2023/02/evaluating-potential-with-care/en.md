We often sum people up as "having great potential" or "being smart," but these two phrases all too easily obscure what really matters: what exactly did we observe, and what makes us believe it will keep holding true in the future?

This article is only my personal framework for observation, not an evaluation system for any organization, and it should not be used as a tool for drawing conclusions about people. A person's development is also shaped by opportunity, resources, health, family responsibilities, and luck; a single performance or a state at one stage is never enough to define a person.

If I must talk about potential, I prefer to look less at static labels and more at how a person acts amid change. The notes below keep my two original angles of observation: one set covers learning, self-drive, thinking, and resilience; the other borrows the external CARE framework to cross-check cognition, action, relationship, and emotion. Both are merely coordinates that help me ask questions, not a scale for scoring people.

## Potential Is Not a Single, Standalone Ability

In one simplified framework, I watch four things: **learning, self-drive, thinking, and resilience**. They are related to one another but cannot substitute for one another.

- **Learning**: not how much knowledge has been collected, but whether new information can be fitted into existing understanding and thereby change the next approach. The key is iteration efficiency: after receiving feedback, can one adjust faster and more accurately?
- **Self-drive**: not being perpetually fired up or taking everything on yourself, but having a sense of purpose, knowing what is worth investing in, and being able to turn goals into actionable steps.
- **Thinking**: whether one can distinguish facts, assumptions, and preferences; whether one can grasp the key constraints from a jumble of information; and whether one can articulate one's judgment clearly so others can discuss, add to, or challenge it.
- **Resilience**: when facing pressure, failure, or exposed weaknesses, whether one can avoid being entirely trapped by momentary gains and losses and recover the judgment of facts and the next step of action.

The first three are usually easier to see: learning fast, doing a lot, and expressing clearly are all conspicuous. Resilience, by contrast, is easy to overlook, because it usually only shows up when things go poorly. Yet in long-term, unfamiliar, and high-friction work, it is often precisely this quality that separates people.

Resilience is not gritting your teeth and enduring, nor is it dressing up exhaustion as strength. It includes acknowledging setbacks, resting appropriately, seeking support, and returning to the problem itself once emotions have settled. A person who can say, "I did judge this wrongly; what information do I need to fill in now?" usually shows more room for continued growth than someone who strains to prove they were never wrong.

## CARE: Putting Ability Back into the Whole Person

The first framework easily focuses attention on "whether things can get done." CARE offers another, complementary angle: beyond outcomes, it also looks at how a person understands the world, how they act, how they get along with others, and how they face their own emotions.

- **Cognition (cognitive rationality)**: judging on the basis of facts as much as possible, being willing to understand different situations, and not treating one's momentary perspective as the whole of reality.
- **Action (effective execution)**: having a sense of engagement and follow-through, able to turn intent into sustained and effective action.
- **Relation (interpersonal warmth)**: treating people sincerely, being willing to understand others, and being able to build trust in collaboration.
- **Emotion (emotional resilience)**: maintaining a sense of responsibility, perseverance, and initiative in the face of pressure and setbacks, and recovering and asking for help when needed.

These four are not four separate "high-scoring traits." Cognition without action easily stays at the level of commentary; action without relational awareness may only be draining the collaboration; and stress resilience, if understood as never getting tired, will harm long-term judgment. Only by putting them together do we get closer to whether a person can work consistently and reliably in a complex environment.

## "Being Smart" Is More Like the Ability to Calibrate Yourself

I am not fond of understanding smartness as reacting quickly, being articulate, or always answering correctly. Those things have value, but they are not enough to indicate whether a person can handle complex, long-term problems.

For me, a more meaningful kind of "smart" is first of all clear self-awareness: knowing what you are good at and willing to do, and also knowing what you have not yet done and currently cannot do. This sense of boundaries is not self-limitation; it gives judgment a reliable starting point.

It also includes two abilities that are not exactly comfortable.

First, **acknowledging that there may be a gap between subjective feeling and objective fact**. A rejection may mean the proposal genuinely has gaps, or it may simply be a difference in resources, timing, or preferences. Only by separating facts, interpretations, and emotions can you avoid turning every piece of feedback into a verdict on your self-worth.

Second, **continuing to act even after your judgment has been overturned by facts**. Not knowing how to get something done does not mean immediately pretending you do; the more reliable response is to find the crux, fill in the missing information, verify the critical path, and then decide whether to push forward, adjust, or stop. Being smart is not about never taking a detour; it is about not wasting the detours you have already taken.

Therefore, what it means for smartness to actually pay off is not producing a polished answer, but gradually turning something unfamiliar, difficult, or temporarily impossible into a problem that can be understood, verified, and advanced.

## Leadership Is an Extension of These Abilities, Not Another Persona

When a person begins to influence a larger scope, these abilities naturally extend into leadership. It does not presuppose being charismatic or good at standing in front of a stage; what matters more is whether one can build credibility in getting things done, treating people, and taking responsibility.

In getting things done, I look at three things: whether one can stand higher and understand a problem's place within a larger system; whether one can see farther and balance immediate delivery with future options; and whether one can think more deeply, filter out noise, and grasp the constraints and costs that truly matter.

In treating people, sincerity, empathy, and a sense of fairness matter a great deal. They are not there to make one appear agreeable, but because information, disagreements, and responsibilities in collaboration can only be voiced promptly within relationships built on enough trust. When personal goals conflict with shared goals, being able to bring the common problem back to the table is more reliable than turning the relationship into a win-or-lose contest.

Finally, there is a certain disposition: having clear goals without being overly rigid; staying optimistic in the face of difficulty without denying the risks; and being willing to take responsibility while also being willing to hand credit, information, and decision-making authority to the right people. A "small ego" here does not mean lacking confidence, but rather not placing the defense of self-esteem ahead of facts and problems.

## Observe Behavior, Not Impressions

If this framework has any practical use, it is not in ranking anyone but in helping us turn vague impressions into more concrete observations.

Rather than asking, "Does he have potential?", one can ask:

- When facing the unknown, does he take the initiative to fill in missing information and form his own judgment?
- When given a different opinion, can he hear the facts and constraints within it, rather than rushing only to defend his position?
- When goals change, conditions fall short, or results miss expectations, can he find the next step anew rather than losing momentum?
- After an experience, does he keep a transferable method that lets him do better next time?

These questions have no standard answers, nor should they be answered on the basis of a single observation. They merely replace "feeling that someone is smart" with a set of behavioral signals that can be verified over time and with care.

This is also my position: potential is not a synonym for talent, résumé, or charisma in expression; it is closer to whether a person can keep learning, calibrate their judgment, take on action, and not lose respect for facts amid pressure and relationships. Resilience matters especially not because people should endure more, but because long-term growth will inevitably encounter uncertainty, failure, and adjustment.

## Finally, Leave Room in Your Judgment

Any assessment has blind spots. An extroverted person is not necessarily more action-oriented, and someone who expresses themselves quickly does not necessarily think more deeply; a person going through difficulty should not be underestimated because of a brief low point. Conversely, a single smooth performance may benefit from a fortunate environment and need not be exaggerated into a stable ability.

So I prefer to treat "potential" as an open hypothesis: it suggests that a person may deserve more opportunities, feedback, and support, rather than deciding for us what kind of person they will become.

What is truly worth cherishing is not pinning on the label of "smart," but the ability, amid ever-changing reality, to still see yourself clearly, see the problem clearly, and take the next step.
