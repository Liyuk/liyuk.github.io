When someone seems to have "lost their drive," the most dangerous reaction from a manager is to immediately explain it as an attitude problem. I have made this mistake myself — when I saw an engineer stop proactively proposing improvements, my first reaction was "Has he stopped trying to get ahead?" Later I discovered that, almost every time, the real cause had nothing to do with attitude.

A person may be losing direction, suffering prolonged overload, seeing no return on their effort, or going through difficulties outside work. Lumping different causes together as "not working hard enough" only makes the real information disappear. Because once the conversation becomes an interrogation about attitude, the other person's next move is to prove their innocence rather than tell you the truth.

## Understand first, don't rush to encourage

When this happens, I open up the situation with three questions instead of rushing to give advice or a pep talk:

- Does he know what problem his current work is solving?
- Is there still a credible connection between his effort and visible growth, recognition, or reward?
- Has his workload already exceeded what he can sustain?

I've seen a case like this: an engineer spent several consecutive months handling urgent issues, and his planned improvement work kept getting interrupted. He began avoiding new tasks and looked "irresponsible." But the real problem was that he no longer believed any promise would be kept — every improvement that was agreed upon would be pushed aside by the next urgent incident. At that point, talking about "ownership" was of no help; the right move was to cut part of the workload, make priorities public, and give him a closed loop of work that could actually be completed.

## Different causes can't share the same solution

Motivation problems have different causes and completely different solutions; mixing them together only fails on both ends:

- When direction is unclear, you need to restate goals and priorities;
- When the role has long been monotonous, you need optional challenges and support;
- When the work environment is unfair or feedback is missing, you need to adjust the mechanisms;
- When the person is going through personal difficulties, you need respect, flexibility, and the necessary help.

A manager can provide opportunities, feedback, resources, and clear expectations, but there are two things a manager cannot do: cannot promise a promotion beyond your control, and cannot turn organizational risk into an obligation that an individual must burn themselves out for. I am especially wary of the latter — packaging a problem the organization should solve as a "growth opportunity" is essentially exploiting the other person.

## Motivation comes from a trustworthy relationship

People are more willing to commit, usually not because they are asked to "be positive," but because they can see three things:

- What I'm doing has meaning;
- My effort will be seen fairly;
- When problems arise, I won't be left on my own.

This is not warm-and-fuzzy management; it is a condition for steady output. First make the problem clear, then discuss goals and actions; first make commitments trustworthy, then expect enthusiasm to return.

## Don't ask "Have you been lacking motivation lately?"

The way you talk also matters. Rather than asking "Have you been lacking motivation lately?" (the other person can only answer "No" and then go on the defensive), it's better to describe an observable fact: "You've completed the last three tasks on time, but you've rarely proposed improvements anymore; I want to know whether it's the work arrangement, your understanding of the goals, or some other factor that has you stuck?"

This gives the other person room to explain and keeps the conversation from turning into self-exoneration. After the talk, agree on just one observable next step and follow up two weeks later — don't expect one conversation to solve everything.

A manager can improve the work environment, but cannot diagnose a person's psychology, much less demand that everyone stay enthusiastic forever. Respecting privacy, allowing "I don't know," and providing reasonable flexibility and channels for seeking help show better boundaries than motivational talk. What a team needs is not high spirits but trustworthy collaboration.
