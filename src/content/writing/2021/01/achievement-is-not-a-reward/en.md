There are already plenty of well-known theories about the sense of achievement. I won't go into theory here; I'll just organize some personal reflections: achievement is not merely the reward you receive after getting something done. It is more like a feedback system that runs continuously, helping you confirm what you are accomplishing, why you are investing yourself, and whether you still approve of the person you are becoming.

> **Revision note**: The original idea for this article took shape six years ago. Since then I have grown more convinced that helping others and "changing the world" are not slogans used to add meaning to work, but things that must be practiced through action. Helping others is a choice within one's reach, but it also requires constantly weighing ability, boundaries, and commitment. "Changing the world" is harder still: it has gradually shifted from a vision into a process of more complexly building and understanding problems, systems, collaboration, and time scales. For me, the sense of achievement lies not in persuading myself to believe something, but in slowly bringing my understanding and my actions into alignment.

## Completing Basic Work Under Pressure

The earliest sense of achievement I felt did not come from doing anything remarkable, but from finishing the work at hand on my own, under pressure.

When I first started working, I was often unsure whether I was up to the job and worried I might not finish things. But when something that originally felt difficult was gradually pushed forward and fully delivered, the feedback was direct: I can do this. This kind of feedback does not have to wait for others to recognize it; it is itself the beginning of self-recognition.

Writing code is a particularly good example. A function, a feature, an implementation whose effect you can see immediately — all of these give instant satisfaction. It is easy to get absorbed in these small, certain feelings of completion, because the action itself produces feedback.

For example, an interaction that was stuck finally flows smoothly, a page goes from blank to usable, a process that kept throwing errors is located and fixed. These are not necessarily grand results, yet they keep answering a very basic question: Can I solve the problem in front of me? For someone just entering the profession, this certainty is especially important.

But this also brings a problem: the stronger the immediate feedback, the easier it is to end up doing only the easy things. Taking on harder problems can of course fail and bring frustration; but if the sense of achievement depends only on "finishing easily," it soon becomes fragile.

So, for me, the first layer of achievement comes from continually keeping commitments: not everything is easy, and not every delivery is elegant, but I am able to push through what needs to be done, even under pressure.

## Identifying With Your Profession

The second layer of achievement comes from identifying with the profession itself.

If you understand a job only as a specific skill, it is easy to be trapped by the boundaries of that skill: what I can and cannot do; whether this thing is worth doing; whether it is useful for my next step. But when I began to understand myself as an engineer, and not merely a user of a particular technology, my perspective slowly opened up.

This means that, beyond the layer I know best, I also began to understand how the neighboring links operate: how the client constrains the experience, how the service provides capability, how data helps with judgment, and why a stability problem becomes the user's problem at a particular moment. It does not require everyone to become an expert in every field; rather, it means no longer treating "this isn't my responsibility" as the end of understanding a problem.

A profession is not only a means of making a living; it is also a role we take on in social collaboration. Identifying with this role means being willing to understand its professional standards, its boundaries of responsibility, and its room for growth — and being willing to learn things beyond skills in order to solve problems.

If a person completely rejects the profession they are engaged in, believing it has no value and no future, then even if they complete many tasks every day, the sense of achievement is hard to sustain. Because they cannot answer: why should I keep doing this?

Professional identification does not demand that everyone love their work; it is the hope that we can establish a coordinate more stable than short-term tasks — knowing what kind of professional we are becoming, and knowing which capabilities are worth accumulating over the long term.

## From Completing Tasks to Understanding Value

One step further, the sense of achievement comes from understanding the value of what you do.

When you are only implementing a piece of logic, your attention easily stays at "did I get it done?" But when you begin to care about whose problem a feature solves, where it sits in the usage chain, and what impact today's trade-offs will leave for the future, work stops being merely the completion of tasks.

I once took part in the iteration of a content feature. A module on the interface is only the final presentation: before it lie creation and upload, storage and review, filtering, sorting, and distribution; after it lie whether readers see it, whether they can understand it, and whether they will keep using it. At the beginning, discussions easily focus on whether the page can launch on time; only by looking forward and backward along the chain do you realize that a seemingly small interaction affects the operational cost for creators, the boundaries of review, and the way readers obtain content. Once you understand these things, you know which details of the experience matter, which constraints must be taken seriously, and whom a single change will affect.

This understanding does not automatically make things easier; on the contrary, it often makes choices more complex. But it lets you know why a seemingly small detail is worth discussing again and again, why some technical debt cannot be dismissed with "we'll deal with it later," and why a solution must not only run but also fit the real situation.

Some people draw their strongest sense of achievement from the technology itself; others draw it from understanding problems and witnessing change. These two paths do not conflict. What matters is not to cut what you do off from the people and problems it ultimately serves.

## Helping Others Without Waiting for Applause

In my professional experience, helping others has always been a very stable source of achievement.

This help can be very concrete: explaining something complex clearly, clarifying a problem together with partners, lending a hand when a colleague is stuck on a difficulty, or writing down your own experience so that those who come later can avoid some detours.

It also often happens in everyday collaboration. In one discussion, there was only a vague idea of "make this better"; we broke it down into what problems users encounter in what situations, which states must be covered, and what counts as effective. At other times, it means working with design to complete how a seemingly simple interface behaves in abnormal states, or explaining the cost, risk, and boundaries of a change to upstream and downstream teams in advance. These things may not appear on anyone's list of achievements, yet they determine whether a team wears each other down or works together to get things right.

Helping sometimes earns thanks and sometimes does not; but the truly stable feedback does not come from the thanks itself. When I decide to help someone, I am already practicing a way of behaving that I approve of: keeping goodwill toward others, and being willing to use my abilities to make things and relationships better.

We cannot rely forever on external evaluation to confirm our worth. A more reliable way is to slowly build a set of judgments of our own: which things are worth doing, and how to do them without betraying ourselves — so that even if no one applauds, we still know the thing is right.

## Believing in What You Are Doing

There is another, harder-won kind of achievement that comes from believing that what you are doing has value.

This belief sometimes comes from trust in your peers, sometimes from your own judgment of a problem, sometimes from real user feedback, and sometimes from an understanding of trends gained through long-term observation. It does not mean blind optimism, and it is certainly not a matter of explaining away every setback as "just hold on a little longer."

Every time I write a plan or discuss what to invest in for the next stage, I ask myself a very simple question: if I really have to build this next, do I believe it is worth doing? Sometimes the answer comes from a trustworthy peer, sometimes from my own long-accumulated judgment, and sometimes from confirmation after real use: a feature I had once deliberated over again and again, once launched, really saved its users a detour.

True belief should allow questioning, allow correction, and allow changing direction after the evidence shifts. It is not stubbornly defending a conclusion, but remaining willing to invest in what you consider important even after seeing the complexity.

"Changing the world" sounds grand, even somewhat unrealistic. But if you shrink it down a little — sparing someone a bit of trouble, making a collaboration smoother, making a service genuinely useful to people — it is not so far away. Many changes leave no dramatic narrative, yet they happen in concrete people and concrete daily life.

I have also been part of a long-running project that, after a certain stage, suddenly began to be used by more people. Before that, it had gone through many repetitions that looked anything but dazzling: filling in details, handling problems, arguing over trade-offs, and pushing forward through uncertainty. Later, when I saw real usage feedback, what I felt was not just the excitement of "the result turned out well," but the sense that all that investment had finally connected with real people. It reminded me that the value of work sometimes lies not in how many tasks I completed, but in whether it ultimately produced a real impact somewhere.

Perhaps the sense of achievement comes from exactly this confirmation: I do not complete, learn, help, and believe in order to get a reward; rather, through these actions, I gradually become the person I approve of.
