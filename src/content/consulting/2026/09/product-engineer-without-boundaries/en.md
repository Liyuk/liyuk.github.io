> This is an edited account of a real consultation, not a verbatim transcript. The conversation is presented in its original order: the client first described the situation, then I worked through it with him. People, organizations, business details, and project specifics have been abstracted, and some repeated facts have been combined. The advice reflects what we discussed; the client and his team have not yet acted on it.

## The client starts with a recent project

**Client:** Most of my work is engineering. Our team does not have a clearly defined product role right now, and my manager wants engineering to take on both product and engineering work. I had said before that I was willing to do more and had talked about moving toward full-stack work. So when this business project came in, I assumed I was supposed to connect the product and engineering sides. Now I am wondering whether one person can really carry all these roles. I am also worried that my way of working may not fit with the team.

The project was tied to a time-sensitive business goal. Operations wanted to launch quickly. After the request came in, we spent several days clarifying it and going back and forth on the proposal. By the time development started, the expected launch was close. Development and self-testing had to fit into a short window, and we had not agreed early on when QA would get involved. I treated both product and engineering as my responsibility, but we had not clarified who was leading and who was supporting.

At the start, I did not know the existing flows or capabilities very well. The requirements document described features, but it did not give enough context on how people would use them or on the edge cases and constraints. We moved ahead with incomplete information. Looking back, I think I was too optimistic about the timeline and the possible impact.

The conflict was between a new feature and benefits users already had. There was not enough time to build the needed capability from scratch, so we temporarily reused a similar existing feature. Several business scenarios shared some underlying capabilities, and users could need the new feature and their existing benefits at the same time. During internal testing, we found that the two use cases conflicted: some users who joined the new promotion could no longer use benefits they already had.

My first reaction was to limit the damage. We could not let the new promotion disrupt the existing business. Operations was more focused on whether the promotion could still run and how to reach its business goal. I felt we were not looking at exactly the same problem. We also had different understandings of launch and internal testing. The demo made the feature look complete, but Operations had a different idea of what the delivered version could actually do.

Once internal testing surfaced a problem we could not work around, we had to change the temporary solution and delay the launch. Other problems came up afterward too. The implementation itself may not have been the main issue. I was more concerned that the product design had missed use cases and that we had not checked the interactions between markets and existing features at the start.

Product work, design coordination, and acceptance testing took a lot of my time. I could not make backend technical judgments on my own, so I had to ask a senior backend engineer to review the situation with me. I could not participate in every backend decision. Someone in Operations was upset for a while. I tend to think that once the problem is fixed, we can move on, but the other person's frustration was still there. I found that hard to handle, and I was not sure how product and operations were supposed to divide the work.

I also have follow-up support and other small tasks. Wrapping up another exploratory project has taken time too. With this new project on top, my attention is being pulled in several directions. I do not know whether I need to be more proactive, whether I lack the skills, or whether there is simply too much work. I want to know what direction to discuss with my manager. Should I keep taking on product-engineering work? If I do, what will be hard and what should I improve? If I stop taking on these business projects, could I return to more focused engineering work?

I am under a lot of pressure. Maybe anxiety and stress can help me grow, but I also want to feel more optimistic and confident. I still have gaps in backend knowledge, product understanding, and cross-team communication. I was in a bad state that day, and I was wondering if I was suited to this role at all.

## I didn't start by answering, "How can I execute better?"

I first acknowledged some of the sound judgments he had already made. When he saw that existing user benefits might be affected, his first thought was to limit the damage. For backend questions he could not assess independently, he knew to ask an experienced colleague. The project had gone unevenly, but he was not simply focused on pushing the feature out. A disappointing result should not erase those concrete things he did well; they also do not mean he should carry every outcome alone.

He asked how he could execute better: should the schedule be longer, should he leave more buffer, should engineers get information earlier? Those are part of project execution. But as we kept talking, I did not start with a scheduling prescription. There was a more basic question underneath: why had engineering taken on this project? What was Operations trying to achieve? Without product resources, who would work with Operations to clarify the goal, the approach, and the resources?

My understanding is that this marketing project did not receive product resources in the prioritization process, while Operations still had a business goal to meet. Engineering was then asked to take on more product and engineering work. The client understood his assignment as becoming a "product engineer," but the organization may have assigned him a set of blended responsibilities without defining the scope, resources, or decision authority that came with them. My reading is that both his willingness and his ability may have shaped his manager's expectations: he had said he wanted to do more and had mentioned full-stack work, and he had done well in his existing work. That is one way to interpret the expectation, not a reason his manager explicitly gave.

So the surface question was how to execute better, but several questions were mixed together: did he want to keep taking on this kind of work? If so, what business result was he meant to achieve, and what people and resources would it take? When scope, time, and risk conflicted, what could he decide, and when would he need someone with more authority to step in? When all of that is reduced to "Maybe I'm not capable enough," it is easy to pile more work onto oneself and miss the conditions actually holding the project back.

## Start with the outcome, then discuss the approach

I kept digging with him: if this project was going to happen, what metric was it ultimately meant to change? Why did Operations believe this promotion would produce that result? What information did product, engineering, and operations each have? Which resources were already available, and which would need to be coordinated?

The point was not to start by asking Operations, "Is your idea any good?" Nor should a feature request automatically become a must-do task. The business team was carrying a target and wanted to reach it. Engineering could start from that desired result, look for a strategy together, and then talk through expectations and practical constraints. The path might be the one Operations originally proposed; it might also involve a smaller scope, a phased attempt, or a different implementation. The discussion should be about how to reach the goal, not about proving whose first idea was right.

If no one could explain the intended result or how to tell whether it had been achieved, there was not enough basis to start execution. The project could wait, or the team could return to the business discussion and clarify the goal first. That is not engineering making the business decision. Without a goal, there is no way to decide how much resource to invest, which path to take, or whether a completed feature has solved anything.

That was the difficult part of this project. It had not received product resources, but that did not automatically give engineering the full authority of a product role. The client needed to work with Operations and coordinate expectations across key partners and their managers: What were the goal and metric? How would scope and timing be traded off? Where would design, development, and testing resources come from? Who needed to be involved, and who could decide? There could be several ways to move forward; we could not assume one solution had to be built in full.

## What does a product manager actually do?

We also talked through the product work itself. A product manager does more than take an operations request, turn it into a PRD, and hand it to engineering. A request often already contains a proposed solution. Product has to ask what problem the business is trying to solve, what outcome or metric should change, and which users will be affected. Only then can the team judge which features, services, or workflows might produce that result. Business partners may bring many requests and ideas; product turns them into strategies the team can discuss, test, and potentially deliver with the resources available.

This is not a universal job description for every product manager. Teams divide the work differently. In this case, the most relevant capabilities were the ability to break a business request into a problem and its assumptions; understand users and their experience; use the outcome and metrics to decide what to do first, how much to take on, and what to defer; clearly document the context, options, scope, trade-offs, and decisions; and coordinate the product, design, engineering, and testing resources needed to deliver. A product manager cannot guarantee the business metric on their own, but should make the goal, assumptions, and trade-offs clear and help the people with the relevant authority make decisions.

Metrics also need guardrails. A promotion might aim to increase participation or conversion, but not at the cost of damaging existing user benefits or an important part of the experience. Product work does not end at launch either. The team needs to look at the data and user feedback, see whether the original assumptions held up, and decide whether to continue, adjust, stop, or seek formal product resources. Otherwise, it is easy to mistake "the feature shipped" for "the problem was solved."

For this client, one of the hardest parts was communicating through conflict: he needed to cooperate and be willing to push back. Cooperation meant understanding the metric and pressure Operations was carrying, then looking for a way to reach the goal together. Pushing back meant being willing to name a disagreement when the scope, cost, timeline, or risk of a proposal did not add up, or when it might affect other users. He should not accept an unconfirmed commitment as his own. The disagreement is about the proposal and its constraints, not the person who raised it. The point is not to prove the other person wrong, but to bring different positions to the same goal and negotiate from there.

Operations may be focused on whether the promotion can meet its target; engineers may see implementation cost, system risk, and quality boundaries first. If they argue only over a feature detail, each side's pressure takes over the conversation. Product's job is to translate that disagreement into questions both sides can evaluate: What result are we trying to achieve? What will users experience? Which paths are possible with the resources we have? What does each path cost, and what risks does it carry? Who needs to provide resources or make a decision? That means being honest about cost without hiding behind it, and not reducing engineering constraints to a simple "no."

An engineer stepping into product work needs to build more than product and system context. They need to practice problem framing, outcome and metric thinking, experience judgment, clear communication and writing, and expectation-setting across people under pressure. They also need room to negotiate and access to resources. Knowing how to write a PRD does not automatically give someone the authority to make every product decision. But if they are expected to move the work forward, they need to be able to discuss goals, options, and constraints with the business, and know which disagreements to take to someone with decision authority.

## Authorization is not always handed to you

We talked about how organizational authority is not always spelled out neatly in a job description. When someone is asked to take on more work, they often have to make the boundaries explicit and negotiate for the room they need to move the work forward. This resembles the work of a senior staff member: rather than only taking a task, you coordinate information, resources, and people to make progress toward an outcome.

That does not mean he can decide everything for his manager or the business. It is more practical to negotiate specific things as the work takes shape: Who do I need to speak with directly to move this result forward? Which scope or implementation choices can I propose and coordinate? Who can allocate product, design, engineering, and testing resources? If a decision affects multiple markets, conflicts with an existing feature, or puts delivery at risk, who decides? When should I ask for help, whom should I go to, and what information should I bring?

This authority may be informal. He may have to ask for it and build it through the work. Still, when accepting an experimental assignment, he needs to understand what he can decide, how resources will be obtained, and when and where to escalate. Otherwise, he will not know when to ask for help or what he can move forward himself. The approach can stay flexible; the boundaries should not be left to guesswork.

The client also said his manager wanted specifics: the people involved and what each person did or decided. My reading was that the manager wanted to identify where the problem occurred and who made which decision, then work on resolving it. In this conversation, the manager seemed focused on solving the problem rather than first taking in or processing the client's feelings. Before the meeting, it would help to bring concrete facts: who set the goal and what outcome they requested, how the request and proposal changed, which cases were missed, when a risk surfaced, who made which decisions, and what decision was needed now.

Being specific is not about finding someone to blame. It is how you help the person with authority see the problem. Whether the manager had product-management experience or understood what this particular product was meant to solve is only a hypothesis; there is not enough information to judge that, and his request for specifics does not prove that he lacked product knowledge. A safer approach is to explain the business goal, user situation, and available evidence, then check whether everyone shares the same expectations for the goal, the role, and the decisions.

## The execution problems still need a review

That does not make execution irrelevant. The team spent time clarifying the request and discussing the proposal, but development started close to the expected launch. Development, self-testing, and internal testing were squeezed into a short window, and QA had not been scheduled early. With too little time, the team reused a similar capability. Several scenarios shared underlying functionality, and internal testing revealed that the new promotion could affect benefits users already had. The interactions across markets and existing features had not been explored early enough, so the team had to change the temporary solution and delay the launch.

The client also acknowledged that he was eager to prove he could do it. He estimated the timeline and impact too optimistically, and did not raise unknowns and risks early enough. That is something he can improve next time: list key user flows and connected scenarios sooner, check that Operations and design share the same understanding of the feature, and agree early on when QA will join and what the launch checks are. But the lesson cannot be reduced to "leave a few more days of buffer." Without a clear goal, resources, and decision path, buffer may only move the same uncertainty to a later date.

If the project is going ahead, the team needs to work backward from the goal and coordinate expectations. If the target Operations is accountable for remains unchanged while time and resources are limited, discuss scope, implementation, and risk together. If more product, design, or testing work is needed, make the gap visible to whoever can allocate those resources. A smaller test, a manual workflow, or a phased implementation may be options, but the business and engineering teams need to judge whether they fit this case. Not every request can be handled safely with a quick hack that barely makes it to launch.

Engineering still needs to assess effects on users and the system. The client was right to flag a possible impact on existing benefits and propose stopping the harm. Operations was also under pressure to keep the promotion moving and meet its target. They were not opposing camps. Good collaboration means laying out the options around the outcome and user experience, not treating engineering as a delivery service and Operations as a source of requests. Under heavy organizational pressure, people may try to shift risk to another role. But there is no evidence that any particular person did that here, so it should not be presented as a conclusion about Operations' motives.

## Build skills through the work; don't try to become a superhero

He also asked whether better product and backend skills would make attention less of a problem. My answer was that stronger skills could reduce the time spent understanding the system, aligning repeatedly, and reworking decisions. I estimated that this time might be reduced by about half. That is an estimate, not a measured result. But when several responsibilities still land on one person at once, attention still has to be allocated. Familiarity cannot replace resources, and stronger skills cannot replace a conversation about priorities and authority.

In the short term, he does not need to aim to "understand the entire backend and become a fully formed product manager." In this case, more reachable things to practice might be clarifying user flows and historical constraints, knowing which connected scenarios need review, discussing strategies in terms of business metrics, and bringing specific issues to people with authority when he cannot decide. Deeper backend expertise can grow through real work. AI can help list risks, related scenarios, and open questions; it cannot decide the business goal for the team or take responsibility for a trade-off on behalf of someone with authority.

We also briefly discussed situational leadership and coaching: someone's experience, ability, and confidence differ from task to task, and the support they need can change too. The client was willing to take on a new responsibility and had engineering experience, but product judgment, backend context, and cross-team negotiation were still new challenges. Repeated setbacks could wear down his confidence. What would help was a smaller next step, the context needed for a real task, someone he could ask for help, and timely feedback, rather than another list of everything he had to master. This is a way to think about supporting growth, not a label for the client. More stress does not automatically lead to more growth.

## What the consultation clarified

This conversation did not decide whether he should stay in product engineering, and it did not assume a single session could change how the team allocates resources. It turned "How do I execute better?" into questions that could be discussed: What is the final metric? Do the expectations of key partners and their managers match? How will product, design, engineering, and testing resources be obtained? What can the client coordinate or decide? When something exceeds his authority, whom should he go to and when? Which execution problems from this project can he improve himself?

That is what I want a consultation to do: put a person's concrete pressure back into the situation it came from, and separate what calls for skill practice from what needs business negotiation, resources, or authority. The answer is not necessarily "keep taking it on" or "quit now." At a minimum, he could leave with clearer questions to take to Operations and his manager.

If you are dealing with work that has landed on you without a clear path forward, bring a specific situation: what someone wanted to achieve, how time and resources were set, which decisions you could make and which you could not, and where the work got stuck. We can start from those facts and sort out what you can change, whom you need to negotiate with, and what is still unknown. A consultation cannot make organizational decisions for you, but it can help you leave with a clearer next conversation and a better way to judge your options.
