> This is the second episode of Consultation Interviews. The person, companies, conference, and business details have been abstracted, and some facts have been combined. This is an edited account of a real consultation rather than a verbatim transcript; the selected exchanges are the ones that moved the problem definition forward, while the notes explain my analysis.

## You may recognize this situation

- You have built an AI demo. People find it interesting, but no one is willing to fund it yet.
- When speaking with an owner or client, you explain models, architecture, deployment, and performance, and the other person gradually loses interest.
- You know AI can change some work, but cannot say exactly which cost it will reduce, which revenue it will increase, or which risk it will lower.
- You want to improve your communication, but “make it simpler” does not tell you what to ask next.

These problems look like a lack of communication skill. Often, the real issue is that the value chain has not been connected: you have shown something that can be created, but not how it enters a real business workflow and produces an operating result there.

## A consultation about AI pre-sales

The person I spoke with had worked in research and development, then spent a long time running communities. He was now involved in AI evangelism, promotional tools, and training. He had attended an FDE conference. FDE can be understood as Forward Deployed Engineer: someone who brings models and engineering capabilities into a specific organization, workflow, and customer problem, rather than proving technical feasibility only in an experimental environment.

There were many business owners at the conference. Their questions were direct: What can AI do for my business? What can a Forward Deployed Engineer actually solve for me? If I invest people, time, and budget, how soon will I see a result?

He noticed a clear difference between people from technical backgrounds and people from marketing or sales. Technical people quickly moved into details: model selection, agent orchestration, context management, and system integration. Marketing and sales people might know less about implementation, but were more likely to talk about the customer’s problem, budget, and result.

The more specifically a technical person explained the details, the less interested an owner could become. Not because the details had no value, but because they had not answered the question at the owner’s level: what does this have to do with my business?

## “Why do people lose interest when technical people explain things?”

**Him:** I tried talking with business owners today, and it was a real challenge for someone from an R&D background. When I watched people from sales and marketing talk with them, the owners seemed to respond better.

**Me:** It is mainly about making money. The result is easier to see when you talk about it.

**Him:** I still need practice. You could clearly feel on site that several conversations might have turned into deals, but the owners’ interest faded as the conversations continued. I need to strengthen my pre-sales and consulting skills.

**Me:** Do you think technical people lack communication skills, or are they starting the conversation from the wrong place?

**Him:** I listened to several other FDEs speaking with business owners. Technical people have a recognizable style. You can tell very quickly who is technical and who is product-facing. We had not received the clients’ information in advance, so being able to handle that uncertainty on the spot mattered a lot.

There are two layers here. The first is expression: can you describe the same thing in language familiar to the other person? The second is more important: do you understand why the other person needs it, and what result they will use to decide whether to continue? This is why an owner may say, “We need something more convenient,” without knowing what is actually inconvenient. They have a pain point, but not yet a defined problem.

Simply replacing technical terms with more accessible technical terms does not complete the communication. “An intelligent AI assistant” may sound simpler than “a retrieval-augmented multi-agent workflow,” but the owner still does not know which task it will change, which human step it will replace, or which metric it will affect.

## The problem is not “lack of convenience”; it has not been defined yet

He later mentioned a specific client: an enterprise-training company with several thousand corporate customers. It was already using digital humans and knowledge bases in its training work, mainly through workflow tools such as Coze. They wanted the system to be more flexible.

That sounds like a pain point, but it is not yet a problem definition. “Not convenient enough” and “wanting more flexibility” are feelings about the current state. The next questions are: which role finds it inconvenient? Which step is most troublesome? How much labor does it currently require? If it becomes more flexible, will the company serve more customers, shorten delivery time, reduce training costs, or make content updates easier?

**Him:** I replayed the conversation on my way back. When I prepare a proposal for them, I still fall into the technical solution. But I should probably discuss the broader trade-offs instead. That might hold their attention better.

That is the difference between a technical proposal and consulting. A proposal answers, “How can we build it?” Consulting first asks, “What do you actually want to change, and what result is worth investing in?”

## The pleasure of creation is not the same as productivity

AI makes it easy to feel the pleasure of creation. A page, script, workflow, or prototype that once took days may now appear in half an hour. It is natural to feel that you have made enormous progress.

But building something is not the same as producing productivity.

An AI demo can produce productivity only after it enters a real business. Someone has to use it. It has to fit into an existing workflow, replace or shorten a step, and be adopted continuously by the organization. Then it still has to answer: did it increase revenue, lower cost, reduce risk, or make something possible that could not be done before?

The middle of that chain can be written like this:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A["AI capability"] --> B["Specific scenario"] --> C["Workflow change"] --> D["Observable metric"]
    D --> E["Business outcome"] --> F["Continue investing or stop"]
```

Many conversations stop at A: the model can write code, summarize, generate images, or call tools. Owners care about E. What needs to be explained and tested is the set of transitions from A to E, not how much more impressive A can become.

## “So do owners only care about how much money they will make?”

**Him:** We came to a question: do owners only care about the final amount of money? Should technical people just talk about returns?

**Me:** Returns are an outcome, but you cannot simply announce a return figure. First understand which part of the business is worth changing and how the owner currently judges that part.

**Him:** So I should not just say, “This will improve efficiency by 30%”?

**Me:** Right. “Improve efficiency by 30%” is usually not a conclusion. It is an undefined wish. Which role, which workflow, against which baseline, and within what scope? What will the saved time be used for? Are you reducing contractor cost, overtime, or delivery delay? Without those answers, an efficiency number has no business meaning.

Owners do care about revenue and cost, but their operating judgment is not made from one number alone. They also consider cash flow, delivery capacity, customer retention, organizational risk, time to value, and whether the result can be reproduced reliably. Technical people do not need to reduce everything to “how much money will this make?” They need to establish a checkable mapping between a technical change and these operating variables.

## Value appears when you take the other person’s position

The surface question in this conversation was: “How do I improve my communication so that people see the value of what I am selling to them?”

But we soon reached a more basic question: whose position are you taking, and whose interests are you trying to serve?

When you negotiate with or sell to a business owner, you cannot stay in the position of “what technology do I have?” Start from the owner’s position instead. What result are they responsible for? What problem are they facing? How far do they need the problem solved? What benefit would make the change worth making? An owner may not want the most advanced AI. They may simply want to hire fewer people, shorten delivery time, serve more customers with the existing team, or make sure they are not missing an important shift.

AI evangelism, training, and promotional content can easily appeal to a different motivation: anxiety about being left behind. Telling an owner that “everyone else is using AI” or that “you will be eliminated if you do not start now” can win attention quickly, but it does not mean a real problem has been found. It sells uncertainty itself.

## Learn to tell the story from a founder's position

There is another important capability here: the ability to tell a founder's story in a pitch. This does not mean wrapping a system in an inspiring vision. It means explaining, from the position of a founder raising money, why the system should exist, who it is for, and how it can become a business.

The emphasis changes depending on who is listening. To an investor, explain whether the problem is large enough, why it is worth solving now, where the initial wedge is, why customers would pay, and whether the solution can be repeated as a larger business. To a procurement-side owner, answer which workflow the system enters, who will use it, what organizational cost it requires, how soon results can appear, and how the company can exit if it fails. An investor is buying the possibility of future growth; a buyer is buying a result the organization can take responsibility for today. These sales logics should not be mixed together.

A founder's story is useful not because it is more emotional than a product explanation, but because it connects technology, market, and operations into one causal chain: what real and recurring problem affects which people; why the current way of working is insufficient; which step the system changes; what result the customer will pay for; and how revenue, cost, risk, and continued use connect afterward.

When explaining a system's value to an owner, ask yourself three questions. If I were the founder, how would I prove this is a business worth investing in rather than an attractive demo? If I were the investor, what evidence would I still need before believing it can grow? If I were the procurement-side owner, why would I accept the switching, training, and experimentation costs today? Together, these questions form a more complete owner-level sales logic for B2B work.

Problem-led selling requires the person making the proposal to admit that they do not yet know the answer, then enter the other person’s system and ask: how does the business run, who carries the cost, where do errors repeat, who can authorize a change, and what result would make further investment worthwhile?

## The actual blocker, and why there is no immediate answer

The actual blocker was not an inability to speak. He had not yet completed three translations:

1. From “what AI can do” to “which part of the other person’s business is worth changing”;
2. From “we want more efficiency” to “which cost, revenue stream, or risk the person actually wants to change”;
3. From “a promising demo” to “a solution that a specific role can adopt, a metric can observe, and an owner can justify investing in.”

This cannot be solved by memorizing sales language because the speaker does not yet have enough facts. He may not know the business workflow, bottleneck, decision rights, budget source, or success criteria. Without them, “AI can increase your revenue” is only a generic promise.

## Listen for the need, then break down the uncertainty

**Me:** When you speak with an owner or client next time, do not prepare a list of AI capabilities first. Prepare several kinds of questions.

**Him:** What should I ask?

**Me:** Start with the business result they want to improve, then ask which part of the workflow is blocking it. Ask how the work is done now, who does it, where the cost sits, and what has already been tried. Finally, ask what evidence would tell them that an improvement is worth continuing.

**Him:** So should I avoid talking about technology entirely?

**Me:** No. Technology appears after the other person confirms the problem. You can say that a certain AI capability may be worth testing, but first make clear what will be tested, what success means, and what failure would cost.

An effective conversation should turn the other person’s vague statement into at least these components:

- **Goal:** which operating result they want to improve;
- **Scenario:** which role encounters the problem in which workflow;
- **Baseline:** how much time, money, and labor the work currently consumes, and what result it produces;
- **Hypothesis:** which step AI might change, and why;
- **Validation:** what small experiment could show whether the hypothesis holds;
- **Decision:** who will decide what further investment looks like if the result appears.

This is the same structured thinking I have discussed before: first identify the question you are answering, then make the evidence, assumptions, trade-offs, and next step visible. The difference is that, with a business owner, the frame cannot begin with technical modules. It has to begin with the operating result the other person is responsible for.

## Use a value map to check your own explanation

Before the conversation, turn the capability you want to promote into a small table. It does not need to become a long consulting document. Four or five columns are enough:

| Capability | Workflow changed | User | Observable change | Business meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Automatic customer-conversation summaries | Manual notes become automated summaries | Sales or support | Fewer hours spent on weekly summaries | Lower delivery cost, or more customers served by the same team |
| AI-assisted issue investigation | Manual case-by-case investigation becomes prioritized diagnosis | Operations or technical support | Changes in first response, diagnosis, and resolution time | Lower churn and support cost |

The table is not for manufacturing an attractive ROI. It is for exposing gaps. If you cannot name the user, you have not found the scenario. If you cannot name an observable change, you cannot validate it. If you cannot explain the business meaning, you are still in the pleasure of creation.

The mapping may also fail. The owner’s problem may not be an AI problem. An AI solution may not be worth deploying. The real bottleneck may be a broken process, unclear permissions, poor data quality, or the absence of anyone willing to adopt a new tool. Saying this out loud can build more trust than promising an oversized return.

## Communication practice is not just learning to speak more briefly

Common advice for technical people is to reduce detail, lead with the conclusion, and adapt to the audience. All three are useful, but they are not enough. A better exercise is to answer five questions after every AI project or demo:

1. Who changed which step of their work because of it?
2. What were the original cost and constraints of that step?
3. What change did I observe, and where is the baseline?
4. Which parts are still my hypotheses rather than facts established through use?
5. If the result holds, who would be willing to invest what in the next step?

Then explain it to someone who does not know the implementation. If they can only repeat, “This is a powerful AI tool,” your value mapping is incomplete. If they can say, “It helps this person reduce this part of the work, and we need to watch this result next,” the real conversation has begun.

## How to practice the next conversation: reconstruct the scene before proposing a solution

My advice was not to memorize another question list, but to practice being interested in the person.

When someone mentions something they care about, follow it first. Do not rush to steer the conversation back to the AI capability you know best, or decide immediately what the problem is. Ask: How do you see it? Why does it matter to you? What happened before it, and what does it affect afterward?

These questions may sound like ordinary conversation, but they are really a way to recover context. If someone says, “It is not convenient enough,” slowly find out who finds it inconvenient, where the friction occurs, how the work was done before, why it stayed that way, and what result made them want to change it. The useful information is often not in the first sentence, but in the person’s explanation of causes and judgments.

This resembles structured thinking, but it is not an interrogation with a table. Listen while keeping several possible explanations in mind. Perhaps the process is genuinely slow; perhaps permissions are missing; perhaps the data is unreliable; perhaps the person simply wants to try a new way of working. The next step is not to choose an answer for them, but to check the possibilities: “Is this what you mean? If not, what is different?”

AI can help here, but it is better at organizing than at performing discovery on site. You can give it your thinking together with what you heard and ask it to separate facts, judgments, hypotheses, contradictions, and open questions, or to turn a messy conversation into several possible causal chains. Which chain is closer to reality still has to be checked with the person involved.

AI has not actually encountered the production environment. It can generate plausible judgments from language, but it does not know which details are habitual phrases, which constraints will appear only after launch, or which problems matter only to the person responsible for the result. Human judgment should not be compressed into an answer for AI to provide directly. It should first identify branches that can be tested, then let reality show which one holds.

The exercise should therefore leave you with a gradual reconstruction of the scene: who encountered what problem in which situation, how the problem arose, what constraints already exist, who has the authority to change it, and what can be tried within the current experience, authority, and system conditions. Anything beyond your authority or capability needs another person, more system design, or a higher-level review.

First define the problem clearly; only then can you know what to solve. Otherwise the conversation keeps expanding, and the more content AI generates, the harder it becomes to return to the real problem.

## Where the conversation stopped

We did not conclude that all technical people should become salespeople, nor did we reduce marketing and sales people to storytellers. A more accurate view is that different roles occupy different positions in the value chain and see different facts. Technical people know capability boundaries. Marketing people understand needs and decisions. Owners carry the investment and the outcome. An AI project moves forward when these three judgments are connected.

The conversation produced a few concrete next steps:

1. Open the next conversation with a business goal and workflow problem, not a list of AI capabilities;
2. Break “we want more efficiency” into role, workflow, baseline, hypothesis, and validation method;
3. Write the value map beside the technical proposal, clearly marking facts and hypotheses;
4. Practice switching levels of explanation: results and investment for owners, workflow and adoption for business partners, capability and boundaries for technical peers.

One boundary matters too: not every AI creation needs to prove business value immediately. Exploration, learning, and creative work can be valuable in their own right. But when you are asking for a budget, pursuing a client, or persuading an organization to deploy a solution, “it can be built” is not enough. You have to answer who will use it, what it will change, how to test it, and how the result connects to the business.

## If you are stuck at “I explain the technology, but no one sees the value”

A single consultation cannot teach you an entire client’s business or guarantee that an AI solution will succeed. It can help you do a few concrete things first:

- identify whether you are presenting a capability, a scenario, or a result already supported by evidence;
- turn “AI will improve efficiency” into a value map that can be discussed and tested;
- design the sequence and questions for your next conversation with an owner, client, product partner, or business lead;
- produce a one-page set of facts, hypotheses, metrics, and next steps.

If you are promoting AI, working as an FDE, moving from engineering into client conversations, or holding a demo that no one is willing to fund, bring the proposal, intended audience, and notes from a recent conversation. The first session will determine whether the problem is expression, business understanding, value mapping, or a solution that is not yet ready to sell.
